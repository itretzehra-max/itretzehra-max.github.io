# Retrospective — FlyRank AI Fluency Internship

*Written for the person I was in Week 1.*

When I started this internship, I told myself I wanted to be an AI governance specialist. That felt like the "serious" path — the one that sounded like a real career, built on policy, regulation, risk frameworks. I picked my case study topics accordingly: an AI Risk Register Generator, a compliance classifier for the EU AI Act. Good projects. Real ones. But somewhere between Week 1 and now, the thing I actually looked forward to doing each week quietly stopped being the policy work.

**What I set out to do**

I set out to prove I could think like a governance specialist — read regulation, translate it into structured rules, build something that made compliance faster for a busy team. FL-06 and FL-07 pushed me to design and build an actual agent, and I leaned into that with the Weekly Review Assistant: a Claude Project that holds me accountable to an honest account of my own week instead of a padded one. I built it, shipped it, and used it every week since.

**What changed**

The shift didn't happen all at once. It happened in the parts of this internship I kept enjoying more than I expected to. Building the portfolio site — writing the HTML/CSS, wiring up the contact form, watching a real Formspree submission land in my inbox — pulled at me more than the lectures on policy-making ever did. I liked the feedback loop of shipping something and immediately being able to see it work. Around the same time, I picked up a financial analysis course on Alison during a slower week, mostly out of curiosity, and found myself genuinely interested in it — not because it was assigned, but because I wanted to keep going after the module ended.

By the time I started the HubSpot CRM course, the pattern was obvious: I wasn't drawn to governance as a specialty. I was drawn to prompt engineering, to CRM systems, to financial analysis, to understanding how tools actually work under the hood. Governance didn't disappear — it became something closer to a responsibility I carry into all of that work, not a job title I'm chasing. Knowing how to use AI responsibly is something I want to keep learning about because it's good practice, not because it's the career I'm building toward.

That's not a failure of the original plan. It's the plan doing exactly what it was supposed to do — giving me enough real, hands-on exposure to find out what actually holds my attention, instead of guessing from the outside.

**What I'd build next**

I'm using the semester break to go deeper into the things this internship pointed me toward: SQL, Power BI, Power Query, risk analysis, and CRM, learning them alongside a next case study — a SQL + Power BI risk-analysis dashboard. I've already set a recurring weekly check-in for this on my calendar, tied to the same Weekly Review Assistant that's been keeping me honest about FlyRank work, so I don't let this slide into "some activity" the way job-search prep did for a few weeks here.

**Three things I'm taking with me**

1. **Interest is discovered by building, not by planning.** I couldn't have predicted the shift away from governance by thinking about it — it only became clear once I was actually doing the work and noticed which parts I didn't want to stop doing.

2. **Honest self-reporting is a skill, not a given.** Building an agent that pushes back on my own vague updates taught me more about my actual work patterns than any of the assignments it was tracking. Vague language ("some activity," "did some stuff") isn't neutral — it's usually covering for something that didn't happen yet.

3. **Shipped and imperfect beats polished and theoretical.** My first demo video ran long, was recorded handheld with no tripod, and wasn't the take I would have picked with more practice. I submitted it anyway. The habit of finishing and shipping the real thing — instead of waiting for the ideal version — is the one I most want to keep.
